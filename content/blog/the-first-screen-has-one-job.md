A visitor opens your website with a small question: “Am I in the right place?” The first screen should answer it before asking for anything else.

That sounds simple. It gets harder when everyone wants a line in the hero: the mission statement, three services, a product screenshot, a list of awards, and a button that says “Learn more.” Put them together and the page speaks loudly without saying much.

## Start with the decision

What should someone understand in the first few seconds? For a studio, it might be _what kind of work you make_ and _who it is for_. For a product, it might be _which annoying task becomes easier_. Write that sentence in plain language before opening a design file.

The headline can have personality. The supporting line should do the explaining. If both lines are clever, people have to solve a riddle. If both lines are generic, they have no reason to stay.

## Show enough to make it believable

A first screen doesn't need to tell the whole story. It does need a little evidence. A real product detail, a strong piece of work, or a useful glimpse of the interface gives the visitor something concrete to hold onto.

When that evidence isn't ready, restraint works better than a stock image pretending to be proof.

## Make the next step obvious

The call to action should follow the visitor's interest. “See the work” makes sense on a studio site. “Try the demo” makes sense when a demo exists. “Contact us” is useful once the page has given someone a reason to do it.

The first screen isn't a poster. It's the beginning of a conversation. Give it one clear opening line, one useful piece of context, and a natural place to go next.
