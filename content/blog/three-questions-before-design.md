The easiest part of a new website project is imagining how it could look. The more useful part is deciding what it needs to do.

Before picking a typeface or moving blocks around a canvas, we like to put three questions on the table.

## 1. Who is arriving, and what are they trying to do?

“Everyone” is not an audience. A founder looking for a design partner reads differently from a customer comparing software. Each person arrives with a different level of context and patience.

Choose the primary visitor. Then write down the task they came to complete. That task is the beginning of the page structure.

## 2. What must they believe before they act?

Most calls to action ask for trust. A visitor may need to understand the approach, see examples, compare options, or check practical details first.

This question reveals what content the site actually needs. Sometimes it's a case study. Sometimes it's a clear pricing explanation. Sometimes it's one honest paragraph about how the process works.

## 3. What can we leave out?

Every extra section asks for attention. If it doesn't help the visitor decide, understand, or move forward, it probably doesn't belong on the page.

Editing is a design skill. A focused site often feels more confident because it lets the important parts breathe.

These questions won't choose the final colors. They do something better: they give every later design choice a reason to exist.
